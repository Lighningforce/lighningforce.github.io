Motor architecture

Components CAD next to the machined hardware

Nozzle Graphite, conical

Single piece graphite with two O-ring seats. 75° converging half angle set by what the lathe could cut, throat near 0.47 in sized for roughly 500 psi chamber pressure, 1.08 in exit.

Forward closure 6061 aluminum

Machined in house with two 0.125 in O-rings and a phenolic heat shield disk. The pressure transducer threads into the center with a quarter inch air gap to keep it off the hot gas.

Propellant grains BATES

Three 3 in grains of the team's own formulation. BATES was chosen for simple casting, a flat thrust curve that avoids pressure spikes, and easy comparison against known motors.

Thermal liner Linen phenolic

0.125 in wall, sized by preliminary simulation and confirmed on the stand. Static fires showed little to no regression, and epoxy bonding outlasted RTV, which cracked late in the burn.

Case and coupler Carbon fiber

Stock carbon fiber tube with a machined snap ring groove, rated to 1000 psi at a 1.1 factor of safety. A 2 in fiberglass lip is epoxied over 1.85 in of the case to couple the airframe.

Retention Snap ring

A snap ring was picked over radial pins because it needs less case wall and leaves more room for a thicker, stronger nozzle. A steel washer spreads the load on larger builds.

Propellant

An aluminized APCP mixed by the team: a bimodal ammonium perchlorate blend of 90 and 200 micron particles in an HTPB binder cured with MDI.

Design decisions

BATES over rod and tube

Rod and tube needs a multistep assembly and a centered core that is hard to hold during the burn. BATES casts in one step and has no thin, brittle features to break in transport.

Conical over parabolic

At 2 in scale the efficiency gain from a contoured bell was too small to justify the machining. OpenMotor showed a 15° divergence was optimal; the flight nozzle kept 30° due to shop lead time.

One piece graphite

Loads at this scale allowed solid graphite instead of a phenolic and graphite assembly. Testing showed it was less brittle than expected, and it is easy to swap between firings.

Static fire

Test stand and range safety How every static fire was run

The motor fires nozzle up into a welded square steel base topped with a steel plate. A 6061 aluminum extrusion frame, joined with 90° brackets, carries motor stabilizers that slide along the frame to align and clamp the case.

The forward coupler drops into a motor coupling threaded onto a load cell and pinned through its housing, so thrust goes straight down into the load cell. Chamber pressure is read through the forward closure port and logged on an Altus Metrum EasyMini.

A static fire passes only if the motor shows no deformation or gas leakage during or after the burn. Afterward the nozzle throat is checked for cracking and erosion, the liner for uneven regression, and the forward closure and insulating disc for damage.

Governing codes

Static fires followed NFPA 1125, NFPA 1127, DESR 6055 for standoff, and Florida Tech's Explosive Safety Plan, with a range operations and safety officer overseeing the day.

Briefing and training

A safety briefing is read aloud before setup. Anyone handling hardware needs current HSI training, including HSDC and rocket motor handling; everyone else watches from the spectator zone.

Igniter discipline

Propellant, motors, and igniters live in the explosive magazine. Igniters go in at the last possible moment and stay shorted until the motor is integrated on the stand.

Firing and safing

Everyone stays behind the perimeter for a 5 second countdown, the controller key comes out if anyone crosses it, and no one approaches until 1 minute after the controller is powered off.

Failure analysis Static Fire #5

What happened
Chamber pressure rose far faster than the case rating and the case failed at the snap ring groove, launching the liner and nozzle hardware.
Root cause
Recovered grains showed backside burning. The epoxy bond burned faster than the propellant, flame got behind the grains, burning area jumped, and pressure spiked. The grains were found outside the liner, consistent with the bond failing first.
Corrective action
No direct epoxy bond between grain and liner. The flight motor uses grains in their ABS sleeve, epoxied into the phenolic liner, which gives a barrier against backside burning while keeping the stronger epoxy bond.

Results and takeaways

  • Flight motor ignited cleanly, flew nominally, and came back reusable with no internal damage.
  • Phenolic liner showed no cracking or bending after flight, an improvement over the static fire motors.
  • Onboard pressure data was invalid (it implied Mach 22.9), so the next build needs a transducer bench check and flight data validation step.
  • Next iteration: cut the 15° diverging section found in optimization and characterize the new propellant batch to tighten the pressure prediction.

Architecture trade

Selected: braced hanging mast. The rocket hangs beside a single strut channel mast and pushes up into an S-type load cell, exactly as it flies. It was the cheapest option and the most accurate.

The A-frame gantry measures thrust the same way, so it buys no accuracy while roughly doubling steel and anchors. It is kept as the backup for very soft soil.

Accuracy comes from the load path. The pre fire state is pure tension for a clean tare, the fired state is pure compression, and the printed guide cradles carry almost no normal load, so friction never enters the measurement.

Script output for a 4.0 in ID case with a 0.155 in wall and 0.125 in pins, designed to 1500 psi (twice peak chamber pressure) with a 1.1 safety factor.

How it works

Design load

The bulkhead force is design pressure times the bore area times the safety factor: 1500 psi over a 4.0 in bore at SF 1.1 gives 20,734.51 lbf on each closure.

Forward end: bond first

The forward closure sits in an epoxied joint, so the script first credits the bond (671 psi shear strength over 2 in of contact, about 16,864 lbf) and sizes pins only for the remaining load.

Pin count: shear vs. bearing

Each pin is checked in shear (650 lbf per 0.125 in pin, from its 1300 lbf double shear rating) and in bearing against the 0.155 in wall at 23,000 psi (about 446 lbf per pin). The script keeps whichever needs more pins; bearing in the case wall governs at both ends.

Row layout

Pins need 2.5 diameters of spacing, which fits 43 around the 4.31 in OD. Pins are split into the fewest rows and balanced across them: 9 forward in one row, 47 aft as 24 and 23.

Net section tension

The case wall that remains after drilling has to carry the full load in tension at 20,000 psi. At the forward row that leaves a 1.78 margin.

The problem

CubeSats still rely on radio links that face signal congestion, bottlenecked data rates, and growing concerns over security. Laser communication raises downlink data rates dramatically, but today's systems are large and consume a big share of a mission budget.

LEDISCO Sat (Laser Emitting Diode Intermittent Signal Communications Operations Satellite) targets a design simple and cheap enough to make laser downlink practical for CubeSat class missions. Dr. Eric Swenson, the faculty advisor and primary stakeholder, set the design constraints, launch conditions, and deployer dimensions.

Non space rated build

Build the prototype from non space rated materials, modeled on a space rated design, so the architecture can be validated on a student budget.

Under $1,500

Keep the prototype under $1,500 to encourage rapid prototyping and wider participation in the small satellite industry.

Autonomous modes

Switch autonomously between sun safe, survival, detumbling, and payload modes, taking ground input only during a LEO pass window.

Light based downlink

Carry a primary payload of optical downlink communication, with radio kept as the command and backup link.

Frame design

The frame is sized to a 30 × 20 × 10 cm 6U envelope. Each plate is cut with X pattern trusses, Ø15 mm holes, and a field of Ø5 mm holes, then tied together by 3D printed supports at the corners, along the sides, and down the center.

Prototype frames are made from acrylic and PLA using laser cutting, waterjets, and 3D printers. The final frame moves to steel and aluminum through machining and waterjet cutting.

STR.01 Vibration

A chosen component survives 5.12 grms random vibration, matching Falcon 9.

STR.02 Center of gravity

Within 4.5 cm of center in X, 2 cm in Y, and 7 cm in Z, so attitude control can recover from tumbling.

STR.03 Thermal

No plastic deformation of the outer frame from −20 °C to 65 °C.

STR.04 Protrusion

Nothing protrudes more than 6.5 mm from the frame when stowed.
